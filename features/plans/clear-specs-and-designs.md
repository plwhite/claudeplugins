## Spec

*What the feature must do, written to stand alone against `### Readability` in `features/FEATUREMODEL.md`. Derived from the input captured above — the `(A<n>)` references point back to the user's answers there.*

### The central requirement

**Specs and designs must be written so that a human can read them and understand them.** Enough structure, and enough context, that someone who was not part of the conversation that produced the artefact can follow it. That is the whole point of the feature, and everything below is subordinate to it.

Concretely, the bar is the two success statements from the issue:

- **spec** — *"a user coming at this with no background can read through the requirements and spec and follow them clearly"*;
- **design** — *"a user coming at this with only a rough understanding of the spec can see what is being proposed"*.

The fault being fixed is not missing content. The specs and designs in question already cover the ground; they fail because they are a grab bag of true statements assembled without structure, assuming context the reader does not have, and diving into detail before the reader knows what the thing is. A change that satisfies every requirement below and still produces artefacts a human struggles to read has failed.

### The supporting requirements

Each of the following exists to serve the central requirement; none is an end in itself.

1. **Split the plan file's requirements into two sections.** `## Requirements` holds the input — the source issue verbatim, staged `features/tmp` material, and the user's answers to questions asked during `/feature-spec`. A new `## Spec` section holds what the feature must do, written to stand alone: clear to a reader with no background on the feature, clarifying ambiguities in the input and filling gaps with explicit proposals. Where the input is complete and unambiguous the two overlap heavily; the spec is still written, and goes somewhat deeper.

   The spec side gets structural expectations, so "clear to a reader with no background" is something an implementer and a reviewer can both apply rather than a slogan each interprets differently. `## Spec` must: state what the feature must do before it states detail; define any term it relies on that the reader would not already know; and be followable without having read the source issue, the `features/tmp` material, or the `/feature-spec` conversation. These are the spec-side counterpart of the overview rule in item 2.

   The review agents must review and check against the split, not merely describe it: `feature-spec-reviewer` reviews `## Spec` as the statement of what the feature must do (reading `## Requirements` as input), and `feature-design-reviewer` traces design coverage against `## Spec`. Without this, the section that carries the reviewable content changes while the checks still point at the old one.

2. **Require designs to open with an overview.** A `## Design` section must begin with an overview of how the design works as a whole, followed by detailed sections as needed, and must be followable by a reader who knows only the requirements and spec — not by one who sat through the design conversation.

3. **Catch unclear artefacts before a human reads them.** Requirements 1 and 2 say how a spec and a design should be structured; this one says the structure is enforced rather than merely recommended. An artefact that misses the central requirement — assuming too much context, or diving into detail before establishing the shape of the thing — must be caught and fixed before it reaches the user, measured against the two success statements above.

   This is stated as an outcome, not a mechanism. Adding clarity criteria to `feature-spec-reviewer` and `feature-design-reviewer` is the user's suggested route and a good indication of what they expect, but it is **not** a requirement — design may find a better approach (A5).

4. **Clarity failures and open questions are not conflated.** A badly structured artefact is not fit for the user to read at all: a clarity fault is a `[rewrite]` finding that the calling skill fixes by restructuring, before the user sees anything. A *clearly written* artefact with open questions is fit to put to the user, as a `[decision]` finding asking for the answers. Under the existing severity rules both deny `VERDICT: READY FOR USER REVIEW`, and this feature does **not** change that mapping, nor the unattended-mode gate that reads it (A3, A6). The requirement is that a clarity fault is reported and fixed as a clarity fault, never surfaced to the user as a question — and that a badly structured artefact is severe enough to deny the ready verdict on its own.

5. **A spec may be revised during design, but only with the user's approval.** Design work legitimately exposes problems with a spec — the canonical case being "this cannot be done exactly as specced, but a cheap 99% alternative is X". `/feature-design` must therefore be able to amend `## Spec` rather than being frozen out of it, but never silently: every amendment is put to the user for approval, whether as "this is uncontroversial, please confirm" or as "I think the spec should change, is this acceptable". An unapproved spec change is a defect (A2).

6. **Record the structure in the canonical feature model.** The plan-file structure lives in `devproc/skills/feature-init/FEATUREMODEL.md` (canonical) and its installed copy `features/FEATUREMODEL.md`; the new section and the clarity expectations must be described there, and every artefact that states the plan-file structure must agree with it.

## Sign-off strategy

This is a prose-only change to plugin instructions, with no runtime code. The risk is not that it breaks — it is that it silently changes the behaviour of every future `/feature-spec` and `/feature-design` run, and that reworded agents regress on checks they used to make. The bar is therefore weighted towards fixture testing and user review of the wording, and light on code review.

One gate matters more than the rest. Every criterion below except the last checks *conformance* — that the sections exist, that the fixtures pass, that the docs agree. Conformance is checkable but it is not the objective: an artefact can carry a `## Spec` section and an overview paragraph and still be unreadable. The **readability gate** is what tests the central requirement, and it is the one that decides whether the feature worked.

- **Testing (reviewer agents) — fixture regression.** The fixture baselines in `devproc/tests/feature-spec-reviewer/` and `devproc/tests/feature-design-reviewer/` carry the plan-file section structure, so they are affected whatever mechanism design picks for requirement 3. **Every** case in both suites is re-run in a fresh agent invocation against the final wording, each passing its `.expected.md` per the pass rule in the suite `README.md`. Any expectation deliberately revised is recorded in the sub-task's checkpoint. Auditable: every case has been run and its pass/fail recorded.

- **Testing (the clarity mechanism) — covered by a test suited to it.** Requirement 3 fixes the outcome, not the mechanism (A5), so the test follows design's choice: if the mechanism is reviewer-agent criteria, at least one new fixture pair (`<case>.md` + `<case>.expected.md`) per agent exercising it, added to the suite and its `README.md` case table; if design picks something else, an equivalent check chosen at design time and named in the sub-task's sign-off box. Auditable: whatever mechanism was chosen has a test that demonstrably fails an unclear artefact and passes a clear one.

- **Testing (skill behaviour) — one end-to-end run.** Fixture tests check the reviewers, not the skills that write the plan file, so the skills get their own check: `/feature-spec` is run once end-to-end on a throwaway feature, and the resulting plan file confirmed to carry `## Requirements` and `## Spec` in the new form; `/feature-design` is then run on it and its `## Design` confirmed to open with an overview. The run is done against a recorded snapshot of `features/CURRENT.md`, `features/PENDING.md` and this plan file — `/feature-design` moves an entry into `CURRENT.md`, which at that moment holds this feature — and those files are diffed back to the snapshot afterwards. Auditable: the throwaway plan file is shown to the user, then `git status` shows no residue of the throwaway feature.

- **Documentation — full.** `devproc/skills/feature-init/FEATUREMODEL.md` updated and `/feature-init` re-run so `features/FEATUREMODEL.md` matches it byte for byte; `devproc/README.md` and `docs/workflow.md` updated to describe the new plan-file structure; `CLAUDE.md` `## Current status` updated by `/feature-end`. Auditable: the canonical and installed `FEATUREMODEL.md` are identical, and no artefact in "Known-affected artefacts" still describes the old single-section structure.

- **Code review — one agent pass at the end.** A single `/review-branch` before `/feature-end`, covering all changed files. Per-sub-task code review is not proportionate for prose changes.

- **Docs review — one agent pass at the end.** A single `docs-structure-reviewer` pass over the changed documentation, performed at `/feature-end`.

- **User review — one consolidated pass over all prose, plus a final pass.** The wording *is* the deliverable, so the user reads and confirms it directly. **Amended 2026-08-08 at the user's request:** originally this was a review per sub-task as each completed; it is now a single pass over every skill, agent and documentation change once they are all made. Reviewing prose that cross-references itself in fragments is slower and less effective than reading it whole, and it forces the user to hold half-finished context between sittings. (Sub-task 1's wording was reviewed separately before this change and stands.) A final user review still confirms the feature as a whole.

- **Readability gate (the one that tests the central requirement) — user judgement on real output.** Before `/feature-end`, the user reads two artefacts produced under the new rules: this feature's own plan file, restructured into `## Requirements` / `## Spec` with an overview-first `## Design`; and the throwaway plan file from the end-to-end skill run above. They are judged against the central requirement — not "does it have the right sections" but "can this be read and understood by someone who was not in the conversation". Auditable: the user confirms both read as intended, or names what still fails. A spec that conforms structurally but does not pass this gate has not met the requirement, and the sub-task is not complete.

  (Restructuring this plan file is the single exception to the no-retrofit decision (A1) — one file, restructured to demonstrate the structure works, not a migration.)

End-of-feature gates for `/feature-design` to materialise as a **"Final sign-off criteria"** sub-task: the `/review-branch` pass, the `docs-structure-reviewer` pass (performed at `/feature-end`), the readability gate, and the final user review.

## Design

### Overview

Specs and designs are written badly today because **nothing in the system says what "well written" means, and nothing checks it**. The writing instructions in `/feature-spec` and `/feature-design` say what a section must *contain*; no instruction anywhere says how it must be *organised* for a reader. So a spec can satisfy every rule in the system and still be unreadable — which is precisely what happened to this feature's own spec, an artefact written under the current rules that passed two `feature-spec-reviewer` passes and was then rejected by the user for burying its central point.

The design fixes this in three places, because the failure has three distinct causes:

| # | Intervention | Failure it addresses |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A **readability standard** written into `FEATUREMODEL.md` | Nowhere states what a readable artefact looks like |
| 2 | **Writing instructions** in `/feature-spec` and `/feature-design` that point at the standard | The writer has no target to aim at |
| 3 | A **clarity check** in both reviewer agents, enforced against the standard | Nothing catches a miss before the user's time is spent |

All three are needed. Guidance without enforcement is the status quo, and the status quo produced the rejected spec. Enforcement without guidance gives the reviewer nothing objective to check against, so "unclear" becomes a matter of taste and the finding cannot be argued with or fixed reliably.

Alongside these, the plan file gains the **`## Spec` section** the requirements call for, sitting between `## Requirements` (the input) and `## Sign-off strategy`. This is what makes the standard applicable to specs at all: today `## Requirements` has to be both a faithful record of what the user said *and* a readable statement of what the feature must do, and those two jobs pull in opposite directions — fidelity wants the issue verbatim, readability wants it reorganised. Splitting them lets each section do one job well.

The rest of this section covers each intervention in turn, then the changes that follow from the `## Spec` split, then what is deliberately not being done.

### 1. The readability standard

A new subsection, `### Readability`, of the canonical `devproc/skills/feature-init/FEATUREMODEL.md` (propagated to the installed `features/FEATUREMODEL.md` by re-running `/feature-init`, which is the only sanctioned way to change it) becomes the single canonical statement of what a readable spec or design looks like. Everything else refers to it rather than restating it — the same pattern `### Sign-off criteria` already uses successfully in that file, and for the same reason: a standard duplicated across two skills and two agents will drift, and the drift will be silent.

The standard has to be **concrete enough to check**, or the reviewer criterion built on it is unauditable and the whole feature reduces to an exhortation to write well. So it is written as six structural tests rather than as an appeal to quality:

- **Point first.** The artefact states what it is for before it states how. A reader who stops after the opening section can say what the feature is for and why it exists.
- **Nothing assumed.** Any term, file, component or convention a reader outside the authoring conversation would not know is defined or linked at first use.
- **Unambiguous.** Two competent readers come away with the same understanding; a sentence open to more than one reading is rewritten rather than left for context to settle.
- **Detail subordinated.** Supporting material sits under headings that mark it as supporting, so the reader can see the shape of the argument before deciding how deep to go.
- **Rationale without detour.** Where a decision had a plausible alternative, the artefact records what was chosen and why the alternative lost — placed so it does not interrupt the main line, either as a brief "we do A, not B, because…" at the point of decision or as a separate rejected-options section. The reasoning must be recoverable without being in the reader's way.
- **Proportionate.** The central point is not outweighed by minor detail. This is the test that catches the "grab bag" failure named in the issue, and the one this feature's own spec failed.

Above those sits the pair of outcome statements from the issue, as the standard's purpose — a spec must be followable by a reader with no background *on the feature*, a design by a reader who knows only the requirements and spec. Both assume a reader who knows the project: the artefact explains the feature, not the project. The six tests are how that outcome is checked; the outcome is what they serve. Where a test and the outcome disagree, the outcome wins, and that is stated so nobody games the checklist.

**Why a checklist rather than the outcome statements alone:** the outcome statements are the real bar but they are a judgement call, and a judgement call is exactly what a reviewer agent applies inconsistently. The six tests give it something specific to point at, which also makes the resulting finding actionable — "the central requirement is stated only in a rider to item 3" can be fixed, where "this is hard to read" cannot.

### 2. Instructions to the writer

`/feature-spec` gains a step that writes `## Spec`, and `/feature-design` has its design-writing step reworded to require an overview first. Both point at `### Readability` in `FEATUREMODEL.md` rather than carrying their own copy of the rules.

`/feature-spec` also carries a target-structure skeleton — the fenced plan-file template it creates — and that gains `## Spec` between `## Requirements` and `## Sign-off strategy`, so the created file has the section in the agreed position. A new writing step without the matching skeleton change would leave the skill contradicting itself, which is the same defect §4 flags for `/feature-design`'s skeleton and preserve list.

`/feature-spec`'s new step defines the division of labour between the two sections:

- `## Requirements` stays what it is — the input, captured faithfully: the source issue verbatim, any `features/tmp` material, and the user's answers to questions asked during the run. Its job is fidelity, and it is explicitly *not* required to be readable in the `### Readability` sense, because reorganising a user's words to read better is how their meaning gets lost.
- `## Spec` states what the feature must do, written to stand alone against the standard. It clarifies ambiguities in the input and fills gaps with explicit proposals, marking each proposal as such so the user can see what they are being asked to accept.

Where the input is thin — `/feature-spec`'s existing branch for a bare one-line description, which leaves `## Requirements` as a pointer to the `features/PENDING.md` summary — `## Spec` is still written, entirely from marked proposals. It is never left as a placeholder: "fills gaps with explicit proposals" is already the section's job, and a placeholder would draw a permanent clarity finding on a path the skill already expects to end in `NEEDS WORK` and a conversation with the user.

`/feature-design`'s reworded step requires `## Design` to open with an overview of how the design works as a whole before any detailed section — enough that a reader who knows only the requirements and spec can follow what is being proposed.

`/feature-design` also gains the **spec-amendment route** that requirement 5 asks for. Design work legitimately exposes problems with a spec, and today `/feature-design` has no sanctioned way to act on that. The new route: where design reveals that the spec is wrong, incomplete, or impossible as written, `/feature-design` may amend `## Spec` — but never silently. Every amendment is put to the user for approval, framed either as "this is uncontroversial, please confirm" or as "I think the spec should change, is this acceptable".

Two details of where the route lives, both chosen to avoid damaging existing contracts:

- **Amendments are logged in `## Review record`**, and `FEATUREMODEL.md`'s description of that section is widened to admit them. Today it is defined as one line per lifecycle stage carrying a review verdict, so an amendment line does not fit; the alternative was `## Handoff`, which was rejected because Handoff is rewritten at every checkpoint and so cannot hold a durable record of what the spec used to say. Widening the Review record's definition is the smaller change and keeps the feature's audit trail in one place. The amendment line is written by the `/feature-design`-specific step, **not** by the shared step-8 record instruction, whose three canonical line forms and cross-skill parity are unchanged — same reasoning as the unattended stop below.
- **The unattended stop is written as a `/feature-design`-specific step, outside the shared review block.** Steps 7/8 of the two skills are near-identical by design and carry an explicit warning against divergence in the unattended gate. A spec amendment is a `/feature-design`-only event, so putting its stop condition inside that block would look exactly like the drift the warning exists to catch. It stops the run on the same principle as a `[decision]` finding — it is a question that is the user's to answer — but it lives in its own step.

### 3. Enforcement in the reviewer agents

Both reviewer agents gain a **Clarity** criterion checked against `### Readability`, and both have their review scope adjusted for the new section.

**Severity and marking.** A clarity failure is rated **MAJOR** and marked **`[rewrite]`**. That combination is what delivers requirement 4, and it is worth being explicit about why, because the two halves do different jobs:

- **MAJOR** denies `VERDICT: READY FOR USER REVIEW` (which requires no BLOCKING and no MAJOR findings). So a badly structured artefact cannot reach the user as "ready" — A3's requirement.
- **`[rewrite]`** tells the calling skill to fix it itself rather than ask the user about it. The skill restructures, calls the reviewer a second time, and that pass normally earns the ready verdict — A6's requirement that clarity faults are handled before the user sees them, "just like any other issue raised by the reviewer".

"Normally", because both skills cap review at two invocations per run. Where that cap is reached with a clarity finding still outstanding, the artefact does reach the user — but it reaches them as `NEEDS WORK` with the fault named as an unresolved clarity fault, not dressed up as a question for them to answer. That is the honest fall-back, and it still satisfies requirement 4's real substance: the two failure kinds are not conflated.

A third change is needed to make the MAJOR rating stick. Both agents currently end with the constraint "Do not flag stylistic preference as MAJOR or BLOCKING". That constraint and a new instruction to rate unreadability as MAJOR do not in fact conflict — an artefact that fails to convey its meaning has failed at its purpose, which is not a question of taste — but an agent that files clarity under "style" will read them as conflicting, and the likely outcome is a quietly downgraded finding. The constraint is therefore qualified in both agents to state the boundary explicitly: taste (wording one would phrase differently where the meaning is already clear) stays capped at MINOR; a failure against `### Readability` is a different category and is MAJOR. Without this the new clarity fixture in sub-task 6 would fail for reasons no one could diagnose from the fixture alone.

No change is made to the severity mapping or the unattended-mode gate; the feature works entirely within the existing machinery, which already routes `[rewrite]` and `[decision]` findings differently.

**Scope changes.** `feature-spec-reviewer` currently reviews `## Requirements` and `## Sign-off strategy`. It now reviews `## Spec` and `## Sign-off strategy`, and reads `## Requirements` as input — with one job retained over that input: checking that source-issue content was genuinely *captured* rather than deferred to, which remains a `## Requirements` property. It also gains a coverage check in the other direction: everything in `## Requirements` must be accounted for in `## Spec`, since the split creates a new place for a requirement to fall through.

`feature-design-reviewer` currently traces design coverage against `## Requirements`. It now traces against `## Spec`, reading `## Requirements` for background. Its existing "a requirement revealed to be impossible is BLOCKING" exception extends to `## Spec`, which is the reviewer-side counterpart of the amendment route above.

**Thin input is still input, and a small feature may pass all the way through.** Raised by the user during Sub-task 2, correcting an error in an earlier draft of this design.

Today a feature specced from a bare one-line description reliably ends at `NEEDS WORK`, and it is tempting to read that as a safety property protecting the unattended gate. It is not. `/feature-spec` writes a placeholder — *"No requirements beyond the summary in `features/PENDING.md`"* — into `## Requirements` whenever the description is a one-liner, and the reviewer then blocks the spec for having no requirements. The skill discards what the user typed and the reviewer objects that nothing was typed. **A one-line description is requirements**: "add logging to the foo function" states what is wanted, and the feature exists precisely because the user said so.

So the split does not break a guarantee; it removes a circularity. Under it:

- `## Requirements` records the user's description **as the input it is**, verbatim. The "no requirements beyond the summary" placeholder goes: it is the one case where the skill wrote something other than what the user provided.
- `## Spec` states what the feature must do, going deeper and marking its proposals.
- A genuinely trivial feature may then earn `READY FOR USER REVIEW` and run all the way through unattended. That is correct behaviour, not a leak. The gate exists to stop work no human sanctioned, not to impose a minimum word count on the sanction.

**No special rule is added for proposals.** A proposal in `## Spec` is judged exactly as anything else: one resting on a judgement the user must make is a `[decision]` finding and blocks; one that merely fills an obvious gap is not. A blanket "every unconfirmed proposal blocks" was considered and rejected — because a thin-input spec is *entirely* proposals, it would make small features permanently unable to pass, which is a defect rather than caution.

One practical consequence, worth stating because it is the real constraint on an unattended thin-input run: the **sign-off strategy** is the part the skill cannot infer from a one-liner. A user who wants such a feature to run start to finish without stopping needs to supply the strategy along with the description.

Two artefacts assert the old behaviour and are corrected as part of this: `feature-spec/SKILL.md`'s thin-input branch and its line ~127 note, and the `NOTES.md` entry "`/feature-spec` from a bare one-line description ends at NEEDS WORK".

**Why not enforce clarity somewhere other than the reviewers?** Requirement 3 fixes the outcome and leaves the mechanism open (A5), so the alternative was considered: a separate clarity-reviewer agent, invoked by both skills. It was rejected because it doubles the agent invocations per run for a check that needs the same reading of the same file the existing reviewer is already doing, and because a separate agent's findings would arrive outside the `[rewrite]`/`[decision]` routing that requirement 4 depends on. The user's suggested route is also the cheaper one.

### 4. What follows from the `## Spec` split

Adding a section to the plan file means every artefact that *states the plan file's structure* has to agree with it, or the next session reads a stale description and writes the wrong shape. Those artefacts are the canonical `FEATUREMODEL.md` (and thence the installed copy), both reviewer agents, `dev-process-manager.md`, `devproc/README.md`, `docs/workflow.md` — and `feature-design/SKILL.md`, which is the most consequential of them.

**`/feature-design` states the structure in three places, and getting this wrong destroys the feature's output.** Its step 5 carries a target-structure skeleton listing the plan file's sections, and an instruction to *preserve* `## Requirements`, `## Sign-off strategy` and `## Review record` when it rewrites the file. If `## Spec` is not added to both, `/feature-design` will silently delete the new section the first time it runs on a plan — losing the very artefact this feature exists to create. Its step 1b also hard-stops when a plan file "has no usable `## Requirements`": that gate deliberately **continues to test `## Requirements` only**, so a legacy plan specced under the old shape stays designable. Making it require `## Spec` would turn the accepted consequence in §5 (a reviewer may report the missing section) into a hard stop that strands every legacy feature — a much worse outcome than the one the user accepted.

Two artefacts named in the spec's "Known-affected artefacts" list turn out **not** to need changing: `feature-checkpoint/SKILL.md` and `feature-end/SKILL.md` neither mention `## Requirements` nor enumerate the plan file's sections — they operate on `## Handoff` and the sub-task list only. This was established by reading them, and it narrows the work rather than changing what the feature must do. Correcting the spec's list is pending the user's confirmation (see `## Handoff`).

#### The test fixtures

The fixture suites are affected far more than incidentally, and they are also the mechanism by which this feature is tested. Three separate kinds of change:

1. **Every fixture file moves with the structure.** Each suite is built on a shared `control.md` baseline that carries the plan-file structure, and every other case is a mutation of that baseline. Concretely: the two suites hold 11 input fixtures and 11 matching `.expected.md` files, plus two READMEs. Both baselines gain `## Spec` and the other 9 inputs follow; the 11 expected files are re-pointed wherever they name a section (see point 2).
2. **Several cases must have their targeted flaw relocated, or they stop testing anything.** `premature-design` and `incomplete-requirements` inject their flaw into `## Requirements`, which the reworded `feature-spec-reviewer` no longer reviews; those flaws move into `## Spec`, and the section names in their `.expected.md` files move with them. Symmetrically, expectations that list `## Requirements` under "Must not report" (`non-auditable-criteria`, and the design suite's `unexplained-design`, which also traces an uncovered requirement that will now live in `## Spec`) are re-pointed. Both suite `README.md` files state which sections the baselines carry and which must draw no BLOCKING or MAJOR finding — both statements become wrong and must be updated.
3. **The baselines must themselves be written to `### Readability`.** This is the subtle one. The suites' pass rule is that no BLOCKING or MAJOR finding may be reported against a section a case does not target — and a MAJOR-capable clarity criterion is checked against *every* section of *every* fixture. The baselines are terse constructed plans written before any readability standard existed, so if their `## Spec`, `## Design` or `## Sub-tasks` text fails one of the six tests, every case in that suite fails, not merely the clarity case. Authoring the baselines to the standard is therefore part of the work, not a happy accident. The alternative — exempting fixtures from the clarity check, or tolerating clarity findings against untargeted sections — was rejected because it would mean the suites could never demonstrate that the criterion behaves correctly on a clean artefact, which is half of what the clarity case has to prove.

- **No retrofit of existing plan files** (A1, A4). The 19 files in `features/plans/` keep their single `## Requirements` section. Nothing needs to tolerate them, because none can be designed again: `/feature-design` only ever designs a feature listed in `features/PENDING.md`, and both that file and `features/DEFERRED.md` — the sole route back into pending — are empty. A plan file with no `## Spec` is therefore an error, not a legacy shape, and is treated as one: `/feature-design` step 1b stops, and `feature-design-reviewer` refuses to review a design against a spec that does not exist. *(Revised 2026-08-09. The design as originally written left both deliberately tolerant, on the reasoning that old-shape plans must stay designable; the user rejected that, since the tolerance protected an empty set while disabling a real check — see the reversed dead end in `## Handoff`.)* This feature is about new features being easier to read, not old ones.
- **No change to verdict semantics or to the severity mapping the gate reads** (A6), as set out under intervention 3. *(Narrowed 2026-08-11, by `/review-branch`.
